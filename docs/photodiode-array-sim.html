Photodiode array motion-sensing sandbox

Drag a light source around to see how aperture size and array resolution shape what a quadrant sensor can and can't detect — and how a mirrored pair of sources can cancel out completely.

How to read this

Left: the sensor plane itself — each colored square is one photodiode. A point light source forms a tight, uniform circle of light on this plane, sized by the aperture; that's the bright circle with the white border. Drag a source to move it — it stays wherever you drop it, so you can compare positions and see the cell readouts update in real time.

Brightness fades near the horizon, on purpose. Instead of distorting the pixel shapes to show field-of-view compression, this fades the source's brightness (and its contribution to the cells) as its elevation angle approaches 90° — a real effect, since a fixed aperture presents a foreshortened, shrinking opening to light arriving at a grazing angle. The dashed circle marks how far the sensor reaches straight-on (along its edges); the square's corners reach a little further, since a square sensor doesn't perfectly match a round field of view.

Aperture size defaults to roughly one photodiode's width — the "aperture the size of one photodiode" setup — but you can go smaller (sharper, dimmer) or larger (blurrier, brighter) to see the tradeoff.

Right: two traces derived from the live cell values — the centroid (what a simple sum/difference sun-tracker circuit reads) and the spread (a shape metric describing how spread out the light is, not just where its center is).

Mirror mode pairs each source with a twin reflected through the center — drag one and its twin moves in exact opposition automatically. This is the worst case for a centroid-only tracker: the two positions cancel in the sum/difference channels by construction, every time, regardless of grid size.

Why 2×2 has a hard blind spot, not just a soft one: in a 2×2 grid every cell sits exactly the same distance from the center. So the spread metric — a weighted average of each cell's distance from center — can't change no matter how light is redistributed among the four quadrants, as long as the centroid stays put. It's mathematically flat, not just quiet. A 3×3 grid breaks this: the center cell sits at a different distance from center than the edge and corner cells, so spread becomes informative again.
